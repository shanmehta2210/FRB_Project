# DM$_{\rm host}$, $z$, $M_*$ and SFR: binned correlations, and a summary of the work so far

Numbers come from `RESULTS.md` (generated by `scripts/binned_correlations.py`) and the other appendix outputs named below. DM$_{\rm host}$ is in the rest frame, $(\mathrm{DM} - \mathrm{DM_{MW}} - \langle \mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle)(1+z)$, the same definition as in the paper.

## 1. Executive summary

**Inclination (main result).** DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on host axis ratio. For all 55 hosts, $r_S = -0.06$ ($p = 0.64$, bootstrap 95% $[-0.32, +0.20]$). The result is the same after removing foreground halos ($-0.04$), for $m_r < 21$, for $n < 2$, and without bulge fits. The FRB inclinations are consistent with randomly oriented control discs: Anderson–Darling $p = 0.44$, also within each redshift bin. We reproduce the anticorrelation of Marnoch et al. (2026) exactly from their table ($r_S = -0.43$). It weakens to $-0.36$ ($p = 0.05$) once their four $q < 0.2$ hosts are dropped, and the combined sample of 69 hosts gives $-0.21$ ($p = 0.08$).

**Foreground halos.** `scripts/foreground_halo_dm.py` estimates the DM added by galaxy halos in front of each host, using the method of Bhardwaj et al. (2025). It reproduces the notebook values for FRB 20240114A exactly once the halo mass passed to the concentration relation is in $h^{-1}M_\odot$; the notebook passes $M_\odot$, which makes its values up to 6% low. The r-band error coefficient has been fixed and all outputs regenerated; the change in results is negligible. The 49 hosts in the Legacy Survey footprint have a median foreground DM of 58 pc cm$^{-3}$ (84th percentile 195; 15 hosts above 100), a median 24% of DM$_{\rm host}$. About 36 of the 49 sightlines are expected to cross at least one foreground halo once the probability that each photo-$z$ galaxy is really in front is included (23 have a crossing galaxy with a spectroscopic redshift). The halo mass function predicts 33.5 of 49 for random sightlines to the same redshifts. The foreground term does not correlate with $q$ ($r_S = +0.14$, $p = 0.33$). Details are in `foreground_halos/WALKTHROUGH.md` (line by line) and `foreground_halos/CODE_CHECKS.md`. One modelling caveat is worth raising: the gas normalization inside $r_{200}$ is a factor of about 3.8 below the stated $f_{\rm gas} = 0.75$ (see `CODE_CHECKS.md`).

**What DM$_{\rm host}$ does track** (`dm_host_physics/DM_HOST_PHYSICS.md`):

- Propagation quantities: $|{\rm RM}|$ ($r_S = +0.49$, $p = 0.001$; the only result that survives the Benjamini–Hochberg correction over 34 tests, $q = 0.04$) and scattering time ($+0.45$, $p = 0.014$).
- Star formation, mostly at $z \ge 0.2$: SFR $+0.55$ ($p = 0.004$) and $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ $+0.50$ ($p = 0.009$).
- Not stellar mass ($-0.01$) and not redshift ($+0.08$).
- The host's own halo is predicted to supply only about 7% of DM$_{\rm host}$ (median 14 pc cm$^{-3}$).

**Comparison with Marnoch et al.** For the 20 shared hosts, our DM$_{\rm host}$ tracks theirs closely ($r_S = 0.99$) but is offset by +62 pc cm$^{-3}$, because the two works use different $\langle {\rm DM_{cosmic}}\rangle$ conventions (`marnoch_recheck/COMBINED.md`).

**Paper.** `paper_56.tex` has three changes to match the regenerated foreground outputs (not yet committed):

- the two foreground rows of the correlation table;
- the median random-sightline term (43 → 44 pc cm$^{-3}$);
- the sentence describing the agreement with the code of Bhardwaj et al. (2025).

## 2. Binned analysis: method

There are four quantities: $z$, $\log M_*$, $\log$ SFR and DM$_{\rm host}$. 46 hosts have all four. Each quantity in turn is the **binning variable** $C$: the sample is split into tertiles of $C$ (14–16 hosts each), and the three pairwise correlations among the other quantities are measured within each tertile. That gives 4 × 3 = 12 panels. Each panel reports:

| statistic | question it answers |
|---|---|
| $r_S$ in each tertile (bootstrap 95%, delete-1 and delete-3 jackknife) | Is the pair correlated at fixed $C$, and is that driven by one to three hosts? |
| Benjamini–Hochberg $q$ over all 36 tertile correlations | With 36 tests, about two reach $p < 0.05$ by chance. Which ones survive? |
| Fisher-pooled within-tertile $r$ and Cochran's $Q$ ("tertiles agree") | Is there one common within-bin correlation, or does it change with $C$? |
| Kendall partial $\tau$ given $C$ (permutation test within tertiles) | Does the pair correlation survive once $C$ is held fixed? |
| DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ Monte Carlo median $r_S$ | Does the result survive the Macquart-relation scatter in DM$_{\rm host}$? |

Tertile edges: $z$ 0.14 / 0.29; $\log M_*$ 9.87 / 10.37; $\log$ SFR $-0.13$ / 0.42; DM$_{\rm host}$ 165 / 254 pc cm$^{-3}$.

## 3. Whole-sample correlations

| pair | $r_S$ ($p$), N = 46 | $r_S$ ($p$), every host with both |
|---|---|---|
| $z$ – $M_*$ | +0.23 (0.12) | +0.32 (0.024), N = 51 |
| $z$ – SFR | +0.34 (0.020) | same |
| $M_*$ – SFR | +0.25 (0.097) | same |
| $z$ – DM$_{\rm host}$ | +0.00 (0.98) | +0.08 (0.55), N = 55 |
| $M_*$ – DM$_{\rm host}$ | −0.04 (0.78) | −0.01 (0.96), N = 51 |
| SFR – DM$_{\rm host}$ | +0.24 (0.10) | same |

The three host-property correlations are positive, as expected. The flux limit means hosts can only be found and measured at higher $z$ if they are massive or star-forming, and the star-forming main sequence links $M_*$ and SFR. DM$_{\rm host}$ is flat in $z$ and $M_*$ and only weakly positive with SFR. The binned analysis asks whether these overall values hide structure at fixed values of the third variable.

## 4. Binned by redshift

![](binned_by_z__log_sfr_vs_logmstar.png)
![](binned_by_z__dm_host_rest_vs_logmstar.png)
![](binned_by_z__dm_host_rest_vs_log_sfr.png)

- **SFR vs $M_*$:** weak and the same in all three redshift bins ($+0.27$, $+0.07$, $+0.22$; tertiles agree, $p = 0.88$). The main sequence is shallow here because the mass range is narrow (the middle third of hosts spans only 0.5 dex) and the SFRs come from three different SED codes. The flux-limit trend with $z$ does not create the $M_*$–SFR correlation: partial $\tau = +0.15$ given $z$.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $M_*$:** the sign appears to change, $-0.29$ and $-0.31$ in the two lower-$z$ bins and $+0.38$ at $z > 0.29$. No single tertile is significant (all $q \ge 0.4$), and $Q$ cannot reject one common value ($p = 0.14$). Pooled $r = -0.06$ and partial $\tau = -0.02$. DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on stellar mass at any redshift; the anticorrelation of Leung et al. (2025) is not seen.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs SFR, the main result of this analysis.** At $z \le 0.29$ there is no correlation ($+0.13$, $-0.12$; $r_S = +0.02$ for all 31 hosts at $z \le 0.29$). In the highest-$z$ tertile ($0.29 < z \le 0.62$, N = 16), $r_S = +0.82$ ($p < 0.001$, BH $q < 0.01$). It is robust:
  - bootstrap 95% interval $[+0.53, +0.93]$;
  - every delete-3 subset gives $p < 0.05$ (minimum $r_S = +0.69$);
  - among the 15 hosts at $z > 0.29$, removing the two highest-DM hosts (both with CIGALE SFRs) leaves $+0.76$ ($p = 0.002$), and Prospector SFRs alone give $+0.80$ ($p = 0.01$, N = 9);
  - after the DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ Monte Carlo the median is $+0.65$.

  The tertiles clearly disagree ($Q$ $p = 0.004$), so the DM–SFR relation is not one law across redshift: it is present at $z \gtrsim 0.3$ and absent below.

**Why only at high $z$?** Three possible explanations, not mutually exclusive:

1. **Noise at low $z$.** At low $z$, DM$_{\rm host}$ is a large fraction of the observed DM, but local structure (group or cluster gas, the FRB's immediate environment) and errors in DM$_{\rm MW}$ add scatter that SFR cannot predict.
2. **Different SFR ranges.** At $z > 0.29$ the flux limit admits only star-forming hosts ($\log$ SFR $-0.4$ to $1.2$). Below, the sample also includes low-SFR dwarfs and passive hosts, where DM$_{\rm host}$ may be set by gas that is not forming stars.
3. **A systematic in $\langle {\rm DM_{cosmic}}\rangle$ at high $z$.** Sightline-to-sightline scatter in DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ grows with $z$. This should weaken a true correlation rather than create one, and the Monte Carlo keeps it at $+0.65$.

The observed signal should be stated as "DM$_{\rm host}$ rises with SFR among $z \gtrsim 0.3$ hosts". It should not be stated as a universal DM–SFR law.

## 5. Binned by stellar mass

![](binned_by_logmstar__log_sfr_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_logmstar__dm_host_rest_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_logmstar__dm_host_rest_vs_log_sfr.png)

- **SFR vs $z$:** positive in every mass bin ($+0.33$, $+0.17$, $+0.31$; agree $p = 0.90$; partial $\tau = +0.20$, $p = 0.075$). At fixed mass, higher-$z$ hosts are more star-forming. This is expected from both the flux limit and the rise of cosmic sSFR with $z$.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $z$:** null at every mass (partial $\tau = -0.00$). DM$_{\rm host}$ does not evolve at fixed mass, consistent with Verdi et al. (2026).
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs SFR:** strongest in the middle mass tertile ($+0.70$, $p = 0.002$, BH $q = 0.04$; delete-3 subsets significant in 93% of cases). It is weaker in the high-mass tertile ($+0.31$) and absent in the low-mass one ($0.00$). The tertiles do not formally disagree ($p = 0.11$), and partial $\tau = +0.19$ ($p = 0.065$). This is partly the same signal as the high-$z$ result: 6 of the 16 middle-mass hosts are also in the high-$z$ tertile. Without them, $r_S = +0.56$ ($p = 0.09$, N = 10). Holding $M_*$ fixed does not remove the DM–SFR link, so it is not a disguised mass effect; it behaves like a specific-SFR or $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ effect, matching $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ in `DM_HOST_PHYSICS.md`.

## 6. Binned by SFR

![](binned_by_log_sfr__logmstar_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_log_sfr__dm_host_rest_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_log_sfr__dm_host_rest_vs_logmstar.png)

- **$M_*$ vs $z$:** weakly positive in every SFR bin (pooled $+0.20$, $p = 0.23$). Most of the whole-sample $z$–$M_*$ trend is carried by SFR, which is also tied to $z$.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $z$:** the signs alternate ($+0.11$, $-0.48$, $+0.37$) and none survives correction ($q \ge 0.43$). Partial $\tau = -0.05$; there is still no redshift dependence.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $M_*$:** the sign drifts from $-0.30$ at low SFR to $+0.14$ at high SFR. The tertiles agree ($p = 0.54$) and partial $\tau = -0.06$, so there is again no mass dependence.

## 7. Binned by DM$_{\rm host}$

![](binned_by_dm_host_rest__logmstar_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_dm_host_rest__log_sfr_vs_z.png)
![](binned_by_dm_host_rest__log_sfr_vs_logmstar.png)

Here the question is reversed: do the host-property relations look different for low- and high-DM bursts?

- **$M_*$ vs $z$:** flat for low-DM hosts ($-0.05$) and about $+0.47$ for the middle and high tertiles. The tertiles agree ($p = 0.31$; pooled $+0.31$, $p = 0.055$).
- **SFR vs $z$:** $+0.56$ and $+0.60$ in the low and high tertiles, $-0.16$ in the middle tertile (agree $p = 0.066$). Partial $\tau = +0.23$ ($p = 0.032$) is the only partial correlation in the grid below 0.05. The SFR–$z$ selection trend survives at fixed DM$_{\rm host}$.
- **SFR vs $M_*$:** the same weak main sequence in every DM bin (agree $p = 0.87$).

Splitting by DM$_{\rm host}$ therefore does not change how the host properties relate to each other.

## 8. Conclusions

1. **Selection trends are as expected.** $z$–SFR and $z$–$M_*$ are positive at fixed values of the other variables. This is the flux limit plus cosmic sSFR evolution, and it is the same for low- and high-DM bursts.
2. **DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on $z$ or $M_*$.** This holds overall, within every tertile of the other quantities, and in the partial correlations ($|\tau| \le 0.06$).
3. **DM$_{\rm host}$ rises with SFR, but only for $z \gtrsim 0.3$ hosts** ($r_S = +0.82$; robust to deleting any three hosts, to the SFR method and to DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ scatter). Holding $M_*$ fixed does not remove it, and it disappears at low $z$. Together with $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ and the scattering and RM correlations, this points to dense, star-forming gas near the FRB as the main source of DM$_{\rm host}$, not the host's stellar mass or its halo.
4. **Caveats.** Each tertile holds about 15 hosts. Of the 36 tertile correlations, two survive the false-discovery correction (DM–SFR at high $z$, $q < 0.01$; DM–SFR at intermediate mass, $q = 0.04$), and they partly overlap. SFRs come from three SED codes with different timescales. The high-$z$ result should be confirmed with a larger or uniformly fitted sample before it is used as a headline claim.

## 9. In plain English

Each fast radio burst comes from a galaxy, and for most of them we know four things: how far away it is (redshift), how many stars the host galaxy has (stellar mass), how fast it is making new stars (star formation rate), and how much gas the burst passed through inside its host (DM$_{\rm host}$). We want to know which of these go together, and above all what makes the host's gas contribution large or small.

Comparing two quantities across the whole sample can mislead, because a third quantity may drive both. For example, distant galaxies in our sample tend to be more massive and more star-forming. This is not because the Universe was like that; faint galaxies far away are simply too hard to find. So if the gas contribution rose with distance, it could look as if it rose with star formation too. To separate these effects, we take one quantity at a time, sort the galaxies into three equal groups by it (low, middle, high), and check whether the other pairs still go together inside each group. If a link survives when the third quantity is held roughly fixed, the third quantity is not causing it. Doing this with each of the four quantities in turn gives the 12 plots.

What we found:

- The host's gas contribution does not depend on distance or on how many stars the galaxy has, whichever way we slice the sample.
- It does go up with the star formation rate, but only for the more distant third of the sample. There the link is strong and survives every robustness check we tried.
- Among the nearer galaxies, there is no such link.

Taken together with the other evidence (bursts with more host gas are also more scattered and more strongly affected by magnetic fields), this suggests the extra gas sits in the busy, star-forming regions near where the burst is born, rather than being set by the galaxy's size or its surrounding halo. The groups are small, about 15 galaxies each, so the result should be confirmed with a larger sample before it is presented as a firm conclusion.
