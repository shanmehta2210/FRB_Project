# DM$_{\rm host}$, $z$, $M_*$, SFR and inclination: binned correlations, and a summary of the work so far

Numbers come from `RESULTS.md` (generated by `scripts/binned_correlations.py`) and the other appendix outputs named below. DM$_{\rm host}$ is in the rest frame, $(\mathrm{DM} - \mathrm{DM_{MW}} - \langle \mathrm{DM_{cosmic}}\rangle)(1+z)$, the same definition as in the paper. The figures of all three main results are collected, with a guide, in `../../major_summary/README.md`.

## 1. Executive summary

**Inclination (main result).**
- DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on host axis ratio. For all 55 hosts, $r_S = -0.06$ ($p = 0.64$, bootstrap 95% $[-0.32, +0.20]$). The result is the same after removing foreground halos ($-0.04$), for $m_r < 21$, for $n < 2$, and without bulge fits.
- None of $z$, $M_*$, SFR or DM$_{\rm host}$ depends on inclination within tertiles of any of the four (Section 8). Only 2 of 48 tertile correlations reach $p < 0.05$, the number expected by chance, and none survives the false-discovery correction.
- The FRB inclinations are consistent with randomly oriented control discs: Anderson–Darling $p_{\rm m} = 0.54$, also within each redshift bin.
- We reproduce the anticorrelation of Marnoch et al. (2026) exactly from their table ($r_S = -0.43$). It weakens to $-0.36$ ($p = 0.05$) once their four $q < 0.2$ hosts are dropped, and the combined sample of 69 hosts gives $-0.21$ ($p = 0.08$).

**Foreground halos.**
- **Method:** `scripts/foreground_halo_dm.py` estimates the DM added by galaxy halos in front of each host, using the method of Bhardwaj et al. (2025).
- **Agreement with the notebook:** it reproduces the notebook values for FRB 20240114A exactly once the halo mass passed to the concentration relation is in $h^{-1}M_\odot$. The notebook passes $M_\odot$, which makes its values up to 6% low.
- **Size of the term:**
  - The 49 hosts in the Legacy Survey footprint have a median foreground DM of 58 pc cm$^{-3}$ (84th percentile 195; 15 hosts above 100), a median 24% of DM$_{\rm host}$.
  - About 36 of the 49 sightlines are expected to cross at least one foreground halo; the halo mass function predicts 33.5 for random sightlines to the same redshifts.
- **No link with inclination:** the foreground term does not correlate with $q$ ($r_S = +0.14$, $p = 0.33$).
- **Documentation:** `foreground_halos/WALKTHROUGH.md` and `foreground_halos/CODE_CHECKS.md`.
- **Caveat:** the gas normalization inside $r_{200}$ is a factor of about 3.8 below the stated $f_{\rm gas} = 0.75$ (see `CODE_CHECKS.md`).

**What DM$_{\rm host}$ does track** (`dm_host_physics/DM_HOST_PHYSICS.md`):

- Propagation quantities: $|{\rm RM}|$ ($r_S = +0.49$, $p = 0.001$; the only result that survives the Benjamini–Hochberg correction over 34 tests, $q = 0.04$) and scattering time ($+0.45$, $p = 0.014$). The AD test ranks them the other way round: scattering $p = 0.002$ ($q = 0.075$), $|{\rm RM}|$ $p = 0.036$. No AD result survives the correction.
- Star formation, mostly at $z \gtrsim 0.2$–0.3: SFR $+0.55$ ($p = 0.004$) and $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ $+0.50$ ($p = 0.009$). With the measurement errors propagated, the binned high-$z$ DM–SFR correlation drops from $+0.82$ to $+0.57$ (Section 4).
- Not stellar mass ($-0.01$) and not redshift ($+0.08$).
- The host's own halo is predicted to supply only about 7% of DM$_{\rm host}$ (median 14 pc cm$^{-3}$).

**Comparison with Marnoch et al.** For the 20 shared hosts, our DM$_{\rm host}$ tracks theirs closely ($r_S = 0.99$) but is offset by +62 pc cm$^{-3}$, because the two works use different $\langle {\rm DM_{cosmic}}\rangle$ conventions (`marnoch_recheck/COMBINED.md`).

## 2. Binned analysis: method

There are five quantities: $z$, $\log M_*$, $\log$ SFR, DM$_{\rm host}$ and the inclination $i$. Each of the first four in turn is the **binning variable** $C$. The sample is split into tertiles of $C$, and correlations are measured within each tertile. Two grids are built:

- **Grid A** (12 panels, `grid.png`): the three pairs among the other three of $z$, $M_*$, SFR and DM$_{\rm host}$.
- **Grid B** (16 panels, `grid_inclination.png`): each of the four quantities against $i$, including the quantity used for binning. The diagonal panels ask whether the relation with $i$ changes along that quantity's own range.

Each panel uses every host that has the values it needs: 46 hosts for panels involving SFR, 51–52 for $M_*$ and 55 otherwise. Tertiles therefore hold 14–19 hosts, and their edges differ slightly between panels. Every point has error bars. Each panel reports:

| statistic | question it answers |
|---|---|
| $r_S$ in each tertile (bootstrap 95%, delete-1 and delete-3 jackknife) | Is the pair correlated at fixed $C$, and is that driven by one to three hosts? |
| AD $p$ in each tertile and overall: two-sample Anderson–Darling test of $y$ for hosts below vs above the median $x$ (5,000 permutations) | Does the distribution of $y$ change with $x$ in any way, including changes that are not a monotonic trend? |
| Benjamini–Hochberg $q$ over the tertile tests of the grid (36 in A, 48 in B), separately for Spearman and AD | With this many tests, two or three reach $p < 0.05$ by chance. Which ones survive? |
| Fisher-pooled within-tertile $r$ and Cochran's $Q$ ("tertiles agree") | Is there one common within-bin correlation, or does it change with $C$? |
| Kendall partial $\tau$ given $C$ (permutation test within tertiles) | Does the pair correlation survive once $C$ is held fixed? |
| Measurement-error Monte Carlo: median $r_S$ [16th, 84th percentile], and the fractions of draws with Spearman $p < 0.05$ and AD $p < 0.05$ | Does the result survive the measurement errors? |

**Measurement-error Monte Carlo.** There are 2,000 draws. In each draw, every quantity is redrawn from its error, the tertiles are re-formed, and all statistics are recomputed:

- **$z$:** fixed for spectroscopic redshifts; $\sigma_z = 0.03$ for the photometric redshift of FRB 20240229A.
- **$M_*$, SFR:** split-normal in dex from the published upper and lower errors, with a floor of 0.1 dex. The median error is used for the three hosts without a published SFR error.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$:** DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ drawn from the Macquart relation and 20% on DM$_{\rm MW}$.
- **$i$:** $q$ redrawn from its fit error (redrawn again if outside $0.2 < q \le 1$), converted with $q_0 = 0.2$, plus a 5° systematic.

AD $p$ tests whether $y$ has the same distribution below and above the median of $x$. Unlike Spearman it is not symmetric in $x$ and $y$.

The host-property correlations are positive, as expected:
- The flux limit means hosts can only be found and measured at higher $z$ if they are massive or star-forming. These trends survive the errors.
- The star-forming main sequence links $M_*$ and SFR.

DM$_{\rm host}$ is flat in $z$ and $M_*$ for both tests. With SFR, Spearman is weak ($p = 0.10$) and AD sees a distribution difference ($p = 0.024$), but both are significant in fewer than 10% of error draws. Nothing correlates with inclination by either test.

## 4. Grid A, binned by redshift

![](binned_by_z__log_sfr_vs_logmstar.png)
![](binned_by_z__dm_host_rest_vs_logmstar.png)
![](binned_by_z__dm_host_rest_vs_log_sfr.png)

- **SFR vs $M_*$:** weak and the same in all three redshift bins ($+0.27$, $+0.07$, $+0.22$; tertiles agree, $p = 0.88$). The main sequence is shallow here because the mass range is narrow and the SFRs come from three different SED codes. Partial $\tau = +0.15$ given $z$ ($p = 0.11$).
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $M_*$** (N = 51):
  - The sign appears to change: $-0.10$ and $-0.41$ in the two lower-$z$ bins, $+0.43$ at $z > 0.29$.
  - No single tertile is significant (all $q \ge 0.42$). Through the errors the values shrink to $-0.13$, $-0.25$, $+0.27$.
  - The tertiles agree ($p = 0.064$). Pooled $r = -0.02$, partial $\tau = -0.01$.
  - So DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on stellar mass at any redshift, and the anticorrelation of Leung et al. (2025) is not seen.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs SFR, the strongest result of this analysis.**
  - At $z \le 0.29$ there is no correlation ($+0.13$, $-0.12$).
  - In the highest-$z$ tertile ($0.29 < z \le 0.62$, N = 16), $r_S = +0.82$ ($p < 0.001$, BH $q < 0.01$).
  - Robustness to individual hosts: bootstrap 95% interval $[+0.52, +0.93]$, and every delete-3 subset gives $p < 0.05$ (minimum $+0.69$). Removing the two highest-DM hosts, both with CIGALE SFRs, leaves $+0.76$ ($p = 0.002$). Prospector SFRs alone give $+0.80$ ($p = 0.01$, N = 9).
  - The AD test agrees: DM$_{\rm host}$ is distributed differently for the lower- and higher-SFR halves ($p = 0.009$). It does not survive the AD false-discovery correction ($q = 0.11$), because with 8 hosts per half the AD test has less power than Spearman.
  - **Through the measurement errors it weakens to $+0.57$ [$+0.37$, $+0.72$], with $p < 0.05$ in 64% of draws (AD: 42%).** The loss comes from the DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ scatter, which is largest at high $z$, and the SFR errors. Hosts also move across the $z = 0.29$ edge between draws.
  - The tertiles clearly disagree ($Q$ $p = 0.004$). Partial $\tau$ given $z$ is $+0.19$ ($p = 0.073$; MC $+0.13$).

**Why only at high $z$?** Three possible explanations, not mutually exclusive:

1. **Noise at low $z$.** At low $z$, DM$_{\rm host}$ is a large fraction of the observed DM, but local structure (group or cluster gas, the FRB's immediate environment) and errors in DM$_{\rm MW}$ add scatter that SFR cannot predict.
2. **Different SFR ranges.** At $z > 0.29$ the flux limit admits only star-forming hosts ($\log$ SFR $-0.4$ to $1.2$). Below, the sample also includes low-SFR dwarfs and passive hosts, where DM$_{\rm host}$ may be set by gas that is not forming stars.
3. **A statistical fluctuation.** With 36 tertile tests, one strong value is not unlikely. The error Monte Carlo keeps it positive in every 16–84% draw but significant in only two-thirds of them.

The signal should be stated as "DM$_{\rm host}$ tends to rise with SFR among $z \gtrsim 0.3$ hosts ($r_S = +0.82$; $+0.57$ through the errors)". It is a lead for a larger sample, not a universal DM–SFR law.

## 8. Grid B: each quantity against inclination, binned by each quantity

![](grid_inclination.png)
![](heatmap_inclination.png)

Rows are the binning quantity; columns are $z$, $M_*$, SFR and DM$_{\rm host}$, each plotted against $i$. If FRB detectability, or the measured host properties, depended on the orientation of the disk, a trend with $i$ would appear in at least some bins. None does:

- **Whole sample:** $\lvert r_S \rvert \le 0.12$ for all four quantities (all $p \ge 0.42$; table in Section 3), and through the errors no pair is significant in more than 1% of draws.
- **Tertiles:**
  - 2 of 48 have Spearman $p < 0.05$, and 2 of 48 have AD $p < 0.05$; 2.4 are expected by chance. Every BH $q$ is $\ge 0.62$ (Spearman) and $\ge 0.96$ (AD).
  - Every partial $\tau$ given the binning quantity has $\lvert\tau\rvert \le 0.11$ and $p \ge 0.21$.
  - Every "tertiles agree" test has $p \ge 0.054$.
- **The nominal hits:**
  - SFR vs $i$ in the lowest-mass tertile ($\log M_* < 9.8$, N = 14): $-0.64$ ($p = 0.013$); through the errors $-0.58$ [$-0.67$, $-0.47$], significant in 74% of draws. The AD test does not see it ($p = 0.22$; 28% of draws). If real, this is most likely dust: edge-on dwarfs are more attenuated, and an SED fit that underestimates the attenuation would give a lower SFR. The same sign appears weakly in the low-$z$ and low-SFR tertiles ($-0.39$, $-0.44$).
  - $M_*$ vs $i$ in the middle DM$_{\rm host}$ tertile: $+0.53$ ($p = 0.027$; AD $p = 0.034$); $+0.39$ through the errors, significant in 28% of draws (AD 30%). Its neighbouring tertiles give $-0.06$ and $-0.04$.
  - SFR vs $i$ in the lowest-SFR tertile: AD only ($p = 0.042$; Spearman $p = 0.087$), significant in 12% of draws.
- **DM$_{\rm host}$ vs $i$:** null in all 12 tertiles for both tests ($\lvert r_S \rvert \le 0.37$, all Spearman $p \ge 0.18$, all AD $p \ge 0.10$). This is the binned counterpart of the main inclination result. For the whole sample, AD $p = 0.19$ for the 55 hosts; in the 51 hosts with $M_*$ it is 0.043, but through the errors that is significant in only 6% of draws, the chance rate.

## 9. Conclusions

1. **Selection trends are as expected.** $z$–SFR and $z$–$M_*$ are positive at fixed values of the other variables and survive the measurement errors (82–92% of draws significant overall). This is the flux limit plus cosmic sSFR evolution, and it is the same for low- and high-DM bursts.
2. **DM$_{\rm host}$ does not depend on $z$ or $M_*$.** This holds overall, within every tertile of the other quantities, in the partial correlations ($\lvert\tau\rvert \le 0.06$) and through the errors ($\lvert r_S \rvert \le 0.05$).
3. **Nothing depends on inclination.** In 16 panels and 48 tertiles, the number of nominal hits is what chance predicts for both Spearman and AD, and none survives the correction.
4. **DM$_{\rm host}$ rises with SFR among $z \gtrsim 0.3$ hosts.**
   - $r_S = +0.82$ (AD $p = 0.009$), robust to deleting any three hosts and to the SFR method.
   - It weakens to $+0.57$ (significant in 64% of draws) once the DM$_{\rm cosmic}$ and SFR errors are propagated.
   - It is absent at low $z$.
   - Together with $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}$ and the scattering and RM correlations, it points to dense, star-forming gas near the FRB as a source of DM$_{\rm host}$, rather than the host's stellar mass or its halo.
   - It is a lead, not a headline: each tertile holds about 15 hosts, the SFRs come from three SED codes, and the result needs a larger or uniformly fitted sample.

## 10. In plain English

Each fast radio burst comes from a galaxy, and for most of them we know five things:
- how far away it is (redshift);
- how many stars the host galaxy has (stellar mass);
- how fast it is making new stars (star formation rate);
- how much gas the burst passed through inside its host (DM$_{\rm host}$);
- how tilted the galaxy's disk is as we see it (inclination).

We want to know which of these go together, and above all whether the tilt matters.

Comparing two quantities across the whole sample can mislead, because a third quantity may drive both. For example, distant galaxies in our sample tend to be more massive and more star-forming. This is not because the Universe was like that; faint galaxies far away are simply too hard to find. To separate such effects, we take one quantity at a time and sort the galaxies into three equal groups by it (low, middle, high). We then check whether the other pairs still go together inside each group. Every measurement has an uncertainty, so we repeat the whole analysis 2,000 times with each value nudged within its error bar. We trust only links that keep showing up.

What we found:

- The tilt of the galaxy is unrelated to its distance, mass, star formation or gas, whichever way we slice the sample. The handful of apparent links is what chance alone produces when this many comparisons are made.
- The host's gas contribution does not depend on distance or on how many stars the galaxy has.
- It does tend to go up with the star formation rate, but only for the more distant third of the sample. The link is strong in the catalogue values but weaker once the uncertainties are included, so it needs a larger sample to confirm.
